How to test

  1. Turn off Night Light, blue-light filters, and adaptive color for a neutral comparison. Use normal brightness.
  2. Enter fullscreen and manually select white, black, red, green, blue, cyan, magenta, and yellow. Use Next or arrow keys to change colors.
  3. Inspect the center and edges of each field for fixed odd dots or regions with a different tint. Compare areas within this screen first.
  4. Automatic cycling is optional; pause it to examine a mark. Record which colors reveal the mark and exit fullscreen after all eight fields.

What counts as normal

Use these references for this visual check. They are not a manufacturer acceptance standard or a hardware certification.

  • Each solid field looks consistent across the visible area, with no fixed wrong-color pixels or large unexpected tinted patches.

How to read your result

If a corner looks pink on the color test screen, note which colors bring out the tint. Repeat the display color test in your monitor’s neutral preset at the same brightness to see whether the patch changes.

A dot refuses to match its background
A stuck or missing subpixel is possible. Confirm with the Dead pixel test and a clean screen.
A solid field looks blotchy
Brightness or color uniformity may vary. Compare different colors and the Grayscale test.
A phone and monitor show different colors
Presets, brightness, white point, color gamut, and profiles differ. A mismatch alone does not mean either panel has failed.

Solid colors reveal visible differences. They do not measure color error, white point, gamut coverage, or create a calibrated color profile.

If you cannot get a result

  1. Disable night and adaptive-color modes, then repeat in a neutral preset at fixed brightness.
  2. If comparing two devices, match their mode and approximate brightness first. Do not treat an uncalibrated device as an exact reference.
  3. Pause the automatic cycle and inspect each field manually. If a mark remains unclear, clean the screen and compare another browser.

Frequently asked questions

Why do the same colors look different on two monitors?

The displays may use different panel types, color gamuts, presets, white points, brightness levels, HDR modes, or color profiles. Put both in a neutral mode and match brightness first. Exact agreement usually requires measuring and calibrating each screen.

Should I turn off Night Light or HDR before an RGB screen test?

Turn off Night Light, blue-light filters, and adaptive color for a neutral SDR comparison. Test HDR separately because it changes the rendering path and brightness behavior. There is no single correct mode for every task, so keep notes on which mode you are checking.

What do red, green, and blue fullscreen patterns reveal?

Primary colors make stuck or missing subpixels easier to isolate. Cyan, magenta, and yellow combine two channels and can reveal tint or channel-specific uniformity. White helps with brightness and tint, while black reveals unwanted light and bright stuck pixels.

Can an online monitor color test calibrate my screen?

No. Solid colors can reveal visible defects and help compare presets, but they cannot measure white point, gamma, gamut, or color error. Reliable calibration needs a colorimeter and software that creates or applies a measured profile.

Can I compare a phone and monitor for exact color accuracy?

Not by eye alone. Each device may use a different gamut, brightness, tone mapping, and ambient-light adjustment. You can spot a large tint difference, but an exact comparison needs both devices in controlled modes and measured against the same target.
