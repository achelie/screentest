How to test

  1. Warm up the display for about 20 minutes. For an SDR check, disable HDR, night mode, and dynamic contrast; set browser zoom to 100%.
  2. Select Black & White Levels. Use the monitor's own settings to compare the 0–5% dark fields and 95–100% light fields; avoid losing nearby steps.
  3. Choose Neutral Grayscale and look for tint. At Gamma 2.2, step back until the checker blends and compare it with the adjacent gray as a rough visual reference.
  4. Use Color Separation to inspect the color fields. Change just one monitor setting at a time, record its original value, and finish by checking ordinary photos or video.

What counts as normal

Use these references for this visual check. They are not a manufacturer acceptance standard or a hardware certification.

  • Near-black and near-white fields remain distinguishable where your display and environment allow, gray looks neutral, and color fields remain distinct.
  • The checker and adjacent gray look roughly similar after they blend. This is a visual guide, not proof that measured gamma equals 2.2.

How to read your result

Dark steps disappear into black
Black level, output range, or contrast processing may crush shadow detail.
Light steps merge into white
Excess contrast or other processing may clip highlights. Recheck after changing one setting.
Gray has a color tint
White balance, color temperature, or a night filter may shift neutral tones.
The two gamma reference areas differ
Display response and browser rendering may differ from this reference. Do not infer an exact gamma value from this comparison.

The page guides visual adjustments. It does not measure gamma, white point, color error, or gamut, and it does not create an ICC profile.

If you cannot get a result

  1. Return to a neutral SDR preset, confirm 100% browser zoom, and compare before making several changes.
  2. Change one monitor setting at a time and keep notes. If the result worsens, restore that value before moving on.
  3. For accurate color work, use a colorimeter or spectrophotometer and measured calibration software. A browser field cannot replace light measurements.

Monitor color calibration FAQ

Can a website really calibrate my monitor?

It can provide repeatable patterns while you adjust the monitor, but it cannot measure the light leaving the panel. Treat this as guided visual adjustment. Verified calibration requires a colorimeter or spectrophotometer and profiling software.

Which preset, white point, and gamma should I use for SDR?

Start with the monitor's sRGB or Standard preset, a 6500K or D65 option, and gamma 2.2 for general web and desktop work. Those labels are starting points, not proof that the display reaches the target accurately.

Why do two monitors still show different white after calibration?

Their backlights, panel types, age, brightness, profiles, viewing angles, and ambient light can differ. Match brightness before judging color. Exact agreement normally requires measuring and profiling each display separately.

Should I turn off HDR, Night Light, and automatic brightness?

Yes for this SDR pass. Turn off HDR, Night Light, blue-light filters, automatic color temperature, adaptive brightness, and dynamic contrast. They change the image while you are trying to establish a stable baseline.

Can this monitor calibration test create an ICC profile?

No. An ICC profile describes measured device behavior. A browser pattern cannot collect those measurements or install a system profile. Use Windows display calibration, the macOS Display Calibrator Assistant, or the software supplied with a measurement device.

When do I need a colorimeter?

Use one when color decisions affect printing, client delivery, product photography, grading, or matching several displays. It is also the practical way to verify white point, tone response, luminance, gamut, and color error instead of estimating them by eye.

Is calibrating an OLED different from calibrating an LCD?

The SDR targets can be the same, but OLED brightness can change with window size and protection features, while LCD results vary with backlight, panel type, and viewing angle. Warm the display, use the normal viewing position, and avoid judging OLED brightness from one tiny white patch alone.

Why can RGB gain not fix a colored area on one side?

RGB gain changes the entire screen. A tint that appears only in one corner or band is usually a uniformity issue, viewing-angle effect, or local panel variation. Use the Screen Uniformity Test to map it before changing global color controls.
