A dead pixel check is simple: show one clean color at a time and look for a point that refuses to match. The trick is removing the usual distractions first.

## Before you start

Wipe the screen with a clean microfiber cloth. A tiny spot of dust can look exactly like a failed pixel on a bright background.

Let the display warm up for 10 minutes. Use its native resolution, turn off any blue light filter, and set brightness to a comfortable level. You do not need to push it to 100 percent.

## Run the color test

Open the [dead pixel test](/tests/dead-pixel) and enter fullscreen. Check these colors in order:

1. Black
2. White
3. Red
4. Green
5. Blue

Stand at your normal viewing distance first. Then move closer and scan the panel in a slow grid. Give extra attention to the corners and the area around the taskbar, since marks there are easy to miss during normal use.

## Read what you see

A point that stays black on every bright color may be a dead pixel. A point that stays red, green, or blue may be a stuck subpixel. A dark smudge that changes shape when you move your head is more likely to be dust, pressure damage, or something behind the panel surface.

Take a photo on two different test colors. Focus the camera on the pixel grid, not on your reflection. The pair of photos gives a retailer or manufacturer much better evidence than one blurry shot.

## Do not press the panel

Pixel massage advice is common and risky. Pressing a modern LCD or OLED panel can spread pressure damage, scratch the coating, or create a larger bright patch. Use the warranty route first.

## What to do next

Check the seller's return window and the maker's pixel policy. Policies vary by panel type, pixel location, and the number of affected pixels. If the display is new, report the issue before the return period ends.

Repeat the test once after reconnecting the display cable or restarting the device. If the same point remains in the same physical place on every input, the panel is the likely cause.
